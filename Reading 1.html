Theory: Twine, the Video Game Technology for All (NY Times Magazine, 2014) & Inspiration: In Defense of the Poor Image (by Hito Steyerl, 2011)

The need for accessibility is always being desired, images like the ones we are told are “the best condition” or “the only way to see it” have become slowly impossible to view, which is where the defense of the poor image is at a rise. Take Broadway plays for example, there are many youtube videos of a certain show that has been prerecorded for audiences who do not have access to go to a theater to view and rewatch. Broadway has become increasingly inaccessible due to expensive ticket prices, so much so that people have relied on lottery drawings to attend, lowering the amount by not that much. Steyerl explains “The poor image has been uploaded, downloaded, shared, reformatted, and reedited. It transforms quality into accessibility …” accessing video and image in a poor image format allows us to be part of the conversation, to contribute to ideas and build on theories. What if images and video were made so that you didn't have to struggle to access it? Would we as a human race become more of a collective? What if films like “Tangerine” directed by Sean Baker that was shot on an Iphone 5s was the standard of movie making? Making it wherever you watch it be a pleasurable viewing experience. Steyerl also argues that the “Poor images are thus popular images — images that can be made and seen by the many.” The poor image creates a sense of reality and also evokes empathy thus making us more connected to each other in a way. There is a similar theme of empathy and reality in the New York Times Article, “Twine, the Video-Game Technology For All” , where an open source digital tool can be used to create “choose your own adventure” and nonlinear story games. Many of the games that Hudson explores reveal personal perspective or experience for a player, almost like hopping in someone else's shoes for a moment. Games like “Depression Quest” created by Zoe Quinn, Patrick Lindsey and Isaac Schankler take players on a journey of what life is like as someone living with depression. Or games like the ones a gamemaker named Porpentine makes which she states a lot of her work is “reclaiming that which has been debased” . These games are on the far end of the gaming spectrum that we rarely see, because much of the games that are popularized center around men. If games did not cater to the patriarchal world, perhaps the way women are seen or addressed could be in a much more respected or valued way.
