Reading 5

Party Artist- Emory Douglas and the Black Panther Party. People’s Art History of the United States (by Nicolas Lampert, 2015)

“The People are the backbone to the Artist and not the Artist to the People. .. the Revolutionary Artist must constantly be agitating the people, but before one agitates the people, as the struggle progresses, one must make strong roots among the masses of the people.” - 1970, Emory Douglas

In 1970, Emory Douglas stated that artists exist to serve the people, emphasizing that a revolutionary artist must stay deeply connected to the masses while inspiring and mobilizing them. As a young artist, Douglas worked for the Black Panthers as a "Revolutionary Artist". He was responsible for all the images, graphics, and posters for their newspapers and overall image of what the Black Panthers represented. Douglas started off drawing political cartoons that depicted all government authority as pigs as a way of showing the black community how police are occupying and highly surveilling black neighborhoods. Along with these visuals, he also included illustrations representing the Black Panthers ' Ten-Point Program. Emory Douglas is an example of how artistry can contribute to movement.

When I look back on my “Lead In Our Bodies” project, I think about ways I can spread the message to continue advocating for my family and neighbors. Art brings people together, and we have a community where we are able to stand stronger. I think about the use of posters and digital media: how can I bring the art to the audience? What ways can I create my art so that when people see it, simply by a glimpse, they can stop and become spectators? How can I make those spectators into participants or contributors? These are questions that will continue to flow in my head while continuing this project, and the message of Emory Douglas about artists being for the people will always motivate my artistry.
